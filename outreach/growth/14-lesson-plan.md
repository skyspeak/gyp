# 14. Personal finance lesson: "Should you pay for a gap year?"

A 45-minute lesson for high school personal finance, economics or advisory.
Free to use. The student worksheet is `14-worksheet.html`; print it.

## Objective

Students compare two ways to spend the same year after high school, one that
costs money and one that pays, and work out the difference in total cost,
including what they give up.

## Standards it fits

Opportunity cost, comparing total cost of a decision, reading published pay
and price information, budgeting. It fits the decision-making and
paying-for-college units in most personal finance courses.

## Materials

- `14-worksheet.html`, printed, one per student
- Optional: https://gyp-psi.vercel.app/programs?ref=lesson on a projector
  for part 3

## Plan

**Warm-up (5 min).** Ask: "If you took a year off before college, what would
it cost?" Take three guesses. Most will assume it costs money.

**Part 1: The priced year (10 min).** Students use the worksheet figures for
a gap year program that costs $43,750. They list what the price covers and
what it doesn't (flights, insurance, spending money), and estimate the full
cost.

**Part 2: The paid year (10 min).** Same exercise for a conservation corps
position paying a stipend. They estimate what they'd have left after living
costs.

**Part 3: Opportunity cost (10 min).** The difference between the two
isn't just price. Students fill in the comparison table: money in, money
out, what each year teaches, and what each one gives up.

**Discussion (10 min).**
- Why do most families hear about the expensive programs first?
  (Answer: advertising budgets.)
- When is paying worth it? Some kinds of year, like outdoor wilderness
  semesters, mostly aren't available as paid programs.
- What would you need to know before choosing?

## Teacher note on the figures

The worksheet uses a real published price and a rounded, realistic stipend,
and asks students to estimate the rest. Before class, check current figures at
https://gyp-psi.vercel.app/programs?ref=lesson. They change yearly, and the
lesson works better with this year's numbers.
